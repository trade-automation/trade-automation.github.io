How to Navigate US Tariffs on Hotel Furniture: Strategies for a 28-30% Tariff Environment

Published August 29, 2026  |  10 min read  |  By foshanFFE.com Editorial Team

The effective tariff on Chinese-manufactured hotel furniture entering the United States now stands at approximately 28–30% — a combination of the base MFN duty (0–5% for HS codes 9401 and 9403) plus the 25% Section 301 additional tariff. With the Section 301 rate scheduled to increase to 30% in January 2027, the total effective tariff could reach 32–35% within months.

For hotel developers, operators, and procurement teams sourcing FF&E, this tariff environment fundamentally changes the cost calculus. But it does not eliminate the economic case for sourcing from world-class manufacturing centers like Foshan, China. This article examines the tariff structure in detail, explores proven mitigation strategies, and explains why Foshan sourcing still delivers a meaningful cost advantage even in the current trade environment.

Understanding the Current Tariff Structure

The total tariff on Chinese hotel furniture entering the US consists of two layers:

For a hotel furniture order valued at $500,000 FOB Foshan, the US tariff adds $140,000–$150,000 in landed costs — a significant but not prohibitive amount when viewed in context of the total cost comparison.

⚠️ Urgency Alert: The Section 301 rate increase from 25% to 30% takes effect in January 2027. Hotel buyers with planned 2027–2028 openings should consider accelerating PO timelines to lock in current 25% rates.

Why Foshan Sourcing Still Wins on Total Cost

Despite the tariff burden, sourcing hotel furniture from Foshan maintains a significant cost advantage over domestic US manufacturing. The reason is simple: the manufacturing cost differential is so large that it absorbs the tariff and still delivers savings.

Net Cost Advantage: 30–45%

According to sourcing analysis from HS YSourcing, direct material costs from Foshan are 30–45% lower than Western domestic distributors. Even after adding the full 28–30% US tariff, the landed cost from Foshan remains 10–25% below US domestic sourcing for most hotel furniture categories.

Strategy 1: Accelerate Timelines to Beat the 30% Increase

The most straightforward tariff mitigation strategy is timing. With the Section 301 rate increasing from 25% to 30% in January 2027, hotel buyers with projects opening in 2027 or 2028 should consider:

  • Issuing FF&E purchase orders before Q4 2026 to lock in the 25% rate
  • Negotiating extended warehousing with suppliers for phased delivery
  • Structuring contracts with tariff-rate escalation clauses tied to PO date, not delivery date

For a typical 200-room hotel project with $1.5M in FF&E, accelerating PO timing saves approximately $75,000 (5% of goods value) — enough to cover design consultation or freight insurance costs.

Strategy 2: DDP Pricing with Full Transparency

Experienced hotel FF&E integrators increasingly quote DDP (Delivered Duty Paid) pricing for US projects. This approach:

  • Gives buyers a single, all-in landed price — no surprise costs at customs
  • Shifts customs classification and duty payment responsibility to the integrator
  • Enables accurate project budgeting without tariff uncertainty
  • Simplifies the procurement process (one invoice, one responsibility chain)

A DDP quote should clearly break down the base manufacturing cost, freight, insurance, and tariff components. This transparency allows buyers to verify pricing against benchmarks and understand exactly what they're paying for.

Strategy 3: Value Engineering Through Specification Optimization

When tariff costs make certain furniture categories uneconomical to import, smart buyers turn to value engineering — adjusting specifications to maintain quality while reducing the tariff impact:

  • Flat-pack / knock-down construction: Ships in smaller containers, reducing per-unit freight and enabling volume optimization
  • Standardized room packages: Reduces SKU complexity, enabling manufacturing efficiencies that offset tariff costs
  • Material substitution: High-pressure laminate (HPL) finishes that look premium at lower cost basis
  • Hybrid sourcing: Import complex casegoods from Foshan while sourcing standard items (mattresses, drapery) domestically

The key insight is that not every furniture category faces the same domestic-vs-import cost differential. Custom millwork, complex casegoods, and upholstered items show the largest cost gaps — making them the strongest candidates for import even at 28–30% tariff.

Strategy 5: Plan for the Long Game

Tariff policy is inherently political and subject to change. The current 25% Section 301 rate was imposed in 2018 and has escalated incrementally since. While further increases are possible, so too are potential trade negotiations that could reduce rates.

Hotel buyers should:

  • Build tariff assumptions into project budgets rather than treating them as fixed costs
  • Maintain relationships with qualified Foshan suppliers to ensure rapid procurement capability when needed
  • Consider the total lifecycle cost of furniture — Foshan-manufactured items with premium specifications often outlast cheaper domestic alternatives
  • Monitor trade policy developments through industry associations and customs brokers

The Bottom Line

A 28–30% tariff is significant. But it is not a deal-breaker. The fundamental economics of hotel furniture manufacturing — labor costs, material supply chains, manufacturing scale, and specialization advantages — still strongly favor Foshan sourcing for most US hotel projects.

With a 30–45% manufacturing cost advantage, Foshan suppliers can absorb the tariff and still deliver landed costs 10–25% below US domestic alternatives. Add the benefits of single-source integration, consistent quality across categories, and the expertise of experienced FF&E integrators, and the economic case for Foshan sourcing remains compelling.

The winners in the current tariff environment are those who plan strategically: accelerating timelines before rate increases, demanding DDP pricing transparency, value-engineering specifications, and partnering with integrators who understand the full cost equation.
