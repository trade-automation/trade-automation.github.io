Smart Room Control & Guestroom Integration

Half of a guestroom control system is installed by the furniture factory. Headboard lighting, mirror-screen lighting, TV-panel lighting, the panel cut-outs and the charging outlets all sit in the joinery. The ceiling box, the riser and the bathroom circuit belong to the main contractor. This page sets out the split, the point heights and the sequence, so the two halves meet during fit-out rather than on the snag list.

What does a guestroom control package include?

A control package is the room control unit, the panels and sensors, the wiring between them, and the scene logic that ties lighting, air conditioning, curtains and door status together. In an FF&E package it is specified alongside the casegoods, because most of the visible hardware is mounted in or on the furniture.

Who installs the lighting that sits inside the furniture?

The furniture factory does. Three of the four lighting domains in a standard guestroom are built into joinery, wired inside it, and tested before the piece is packed. The bathroom is the only domain that belongs entirely to the site trades. The drawing below is the split we work to.

Figure 1. A standard guestroom, four lighting domains, one bus. The FF&E factory owns the harness inside each furniture-mounted domain. The contractor owns the connector above the ceiling.

Where the sequence breaks. The three furniture-mounted domains leave the factory with a plug-in harness. The contractor's only obligation is a matching connector above the ceiling at 2700 mm. When that connector is missing, on the wrong wall or 200 mm out, the panel has to come off after handover to reach the joint, and the furniture has to be re-tested on site.

What has to be built into the joinery?

A control system specified after the casegoods are drawn is a control system installed after the casegoods are finished. These are the details that have to be on the shop drawing, not decided in the room:

  • Headboard. A wiring void behind the upholstered panel, a removable access panel or service hatch, a cut-out for each reading light, and clearance for the LED driver so it is not sitting against foam.
  • Nightstand. The wireless charging pad is recessed into the top, typically a 1 mm engrave so the phone sits flush rather than perched on a plate. USB-C PD and USB-A modules are machined into the same carcass, and the switched socket has to be reachable without moving the unit.
  • Mirror and mirror screen. Back-box depth for the driver and the screen controller, moisture sealing on the bathroom side, and a route to replace the driver without removing the mirror.
  • TV panel and floating shelf. Concealed socket and two data outlets at 1600 mm, cable route inside the panel, ventilation for the media hub if one is specified.
  • Wardrobe. Interior light on a door switch, with the driver in a compartment that can be opened.
  • Door and frame. Door contact recessed into the frame, doorbell and room status panel cut-out at 1500 mm on the corridor face, and a feed from the corridor side that does not depend on the guest being in the room.

The one distinction that causes most callbacks is switched versus unswitched power. The minibar runs on unswitched power and stays live with the card out. Everything a guest can turn off runs on switched power. Wire it the other way round and either the fridge goes warm overnight or the room never goes fully dark.

How does a guest actually use the room?

Scene programming is the part of the package a guest experiences directly and a value-engineering review cuts first. Five of the scenes do most of the work:

SceneWhat it switches
Pre-arrival coolingFront desk marks the room occupied and the air conditioning starts, so the room is at setpoint before the guest opens the door
WelcomeCorridor light on as the door opens, curtains open, welcome message and background music on the room speakers
SleepTV off, curtains closed, lighting down to the night circuit, air conditioning to the sleep setpoint and fresh air running
Night wakeA floor-level or under-bed circuit at low output, so the guest can find the bathroom without turning on the room
MorningCurtains open on schedule or on alarm, lighting ramps, music starts

Two more rules sit behind those scenes. Card out steps the setpoint up and switches off after a timeout, and the room reports its status to the front desk, so housekeeping stops knocking on doors that are already empty. The scene list is usually written at commissioning rather than in the specification, which is why scene behaviour often differs between floors of the same hotel.

What sits behind the wall?

The controller is a modular board, not a fixed black box. Board size follows the number of switched circuits: a standard room usually lands on a 12-way board, a suite with a separate living-area zone on 17 or 21. Air conditioning runs through a dedicated fan-coil module rather than through lighting relays.

  • Network. Rooms sit on TCP/IP to a floor switch. Configuration is browser-based and can be pushed room by room from the front office or from off site, which matters when a scene needs changing across 300 rooms.
  • Logs. The platform keeps a room status log and a controller log. When a guest reports that the bathroom light will not come on, the log says whether the panel, the relay or the lamp is at fault before anyone walks up.
  • Door lock and PMS. Card or face recognition at the door plate feeds the same status the front desk sees, and the check-in record drives pre-arrival cooling.
  • Emergency call. A bathroom pull cord or panel button raises an alarm on the front office screen, as a phone alert to named staff, and by SMS.

The control hardware is produced on a shorter cycle than the casegoods, so it rides the same container as the furniture rather than shipping as a separate consignment. That matters for more than freight: the panels and the joinery they mount into arrive together, and the factory harness has already been mated to a sample panel before either is packed.

Do the panels have to match the joinery finishes?

Yes, and this is the detail most often missed. A switch plate is a visible surface in the same frame as the timber, the stone and the metal, at a distance a guest actually stands from it. Panel families run from large-plate touch glass to metal-framed and dot-touch plates, in the finishes the interior designer selects for the room. A champagne-gold plate set against a black-titanium door frame reads as a mistake at one metre.

  • Finish selection timing. Panel finish is chosen with the FF&E finish schedule, not after it. Late selection is what produces a plate that matches nothing.
  • Cut-out dimensions. Plate dimensions and back-box depths go onto the millwork shop drawing before the panel is machined. A 2 mm error in a routed opening is visible on a flush-mounted plate.
  • Door plates. Room number plates are made to the operator's brand standard, including face-recognition versions where the brand allows it.

Questions buyers ask before specifying

Can smart control be added to a package already in production?

Not without rework. Panel cut-outs, the wiring void in the headboard and the recess in the nightstand top are machined before assembly. If the casegoods are already finished, the practical route is surface-mounted plates and an external driver box, which costs more per room and looks like what it is. The cheaper decision point is the shop drawing stage.

Does the FF&E factory install the control system?

It installs the part that lives inside the furniture: lighting, harness, panel mounting and the charging modules, tested room by room before packing. The controller, the floor network, the bathroom circuit and the commissioning are done on site by the control vendor and the main contractor. foshanFFE coordinates the two sides against one point schedule.

What happens if the contractor's connector does not match the furniture harness?

The furniture has to be opened on site. That is why the harness drawing is issued with the package and why the connector is specified, not assumed. Where a project cannot guarantee the contractor side, we ship a short adapter lead and a labelled terminal block with each room set.

Does a hotel need a full control system to specify this?

No. A room with ordinary switches still needs the wiring void, the cut-outs and the point heights, because the joinery has to be built for whatever goes in later. Specifying the physical provisions now and the electronics later is a normal sequence on phased refurbishments.

How does control affect the FF&E lead time?

The hardware is not the constraint; the drawing is. Panels, plate finishes and cut-out dimensions have to be frozen before the joinery is machined, and that approval sits in the same shop drawing cycle as the rest of the casegoods. Once approved, the control hardware rides the same production window and the same container as the furniture.

Which markets does this work in?

The electrical standard, plug type and voltage change by market, and the panels are made to the destination standard rather than adapted afterwards. Middle East and North American projects are quoted with the socket configuration and certification the local authority requires, confirmed at quotation rather than at delivery.

Where this sits in the package

Control provisions are specified with the guestroom FF&E package, because they are built into the same casegoods. They are tested under the same inspection regime as the furniture, room by room, before packing. They ship in the same consolidated container program, so panels and joinery arrive together.
