Contract Seating

Seating is the only FF&E item a guest touches with their whole body for an hour at a time. This page is the specification behind it — the numbers a booth has to hit, the foam and rub count each zone needs, and how the frame is jointed. The current range is photographed on the F&B page.

What has to be settled before a seating schedule can be priced?

A schedule that lists model numbers and quantities cannot be quoted. Six items decide the price, and five of them usually arrive after the drawing does.

DecisionWhat it changes
Seat height against table heightA 450 mm seat under a 750 mm top is the working difference. Drop the table 25 mm and every guest sits with their elbows up; raise it and the seat becomes a perch. The pair is one decision, not two.
Booth depth and pitchA single booth runs 1,200–1,300 mm overall, back-to-back 1,900–2,100 mm, with 600–650 mm of pitch per cover. Fifty millimetres short and the bay loses a cover it was drawn to hold.
Who supplies the fabricCOM and COL add waste and calendar. A patterned fabric with a 320 mm repeat can consume a third more than a plain weave on the same chair, and the cutting report is what exposes it.
Rub count by zone25,000 Martindale is a guestroom chair. A restaurant floor needs 40,000. A lobby or bar runs 50,000–100,000. Specifying one fabric across all three is how a lobby looks tired at year two.
Frame and fixingA banquette bolted to a screed floor and one standing on adjustable feet are different products at the same price on paper. So is a chair that has to stack to 8 high in a ballroom.
Destination complianceCAL 117 for California, BS 7177 for the UK, IMO where anything goes on a vessel. The same chair is three different builds, and the foam is what changes.

What are the numbers that make a booth work?

These are the dimensions a fixed seating drawing has to carry. They are not ours — they are the working set the industry draws to — but they are the ones that get argued about on site, so they are worth putting on the page.

DimensionTypicalWhat breaks when it is wrong
Seat height450 mmUnder 430 mm the seat reads low and guests slide forward all evening; over 470 mm the table cuts across the forearm.
Table top height750 mmFixed once, then the booth is fixed too. Change one and both have to be re-cut.
Seat to table difference300 mmThe single number that decides whether a guest can rest a forearm without lifting a shoulder.
Seat depth450–500 mmDeeper and short guests cannot reach the back; shallower and the back does no work.
Back height above finish floor1,000–1,100 mmBelow 900 mm the bay stops dividing the room and the noise from the next table arrives with the food.
Single booth, overall depth1,200–1,300 mmDrawn at 1,150 to fit a wall and every guest in the bay sits forward of the back.
Back-to-back double booth1,900–2,100 mmThe shared back panel is where two bays either align or fight each other on the floor plan.
Cover pitch along a run600–650 mmThe wall length divided by the cover count is not a pitch. It is how a bay loses a seat.
Service aisle1,200 mm, 900 mm accessibleUnder 1,100 mm a tray cannot pass a seated guest without an apology.
Knee clearance600 mm wide × 650 mm highThe pedestal and the table apron are decided here, and both are already ordered by the time anyone checks.
The error that repeats. A booth drawn to fit the room instead of the room drawn to fit the booth. It photographs fine on a plan and shows up in the first week of service, when every guest in the bay is sitting on the front third of the seat. The fix is not a new back cushion; it is a new plinth.

Which foam and which fabric survive which zone?

Seating fails from the inside out. The frame rarely breaks; what collapses is the seat foam, what splits is the vinyl at the piping, and what works loose is the ferrule on a leg that gets dragged across tile twice a service.

ElementWhat contract grade means
Seat foamMoulded high-resilience foam at 35–40 kg/m³ over a plywood or steel sub-frame. The block foam in a residential dining chair is the reason a restaurant seat has a hollow at month eight.
Back foam25–30 kg/m³ is enough where the back only leans; a booth back that gets pushed against all night wants the seat spec.
Rub count25,000 Martindale for guestroom and suite chairs, 40,000 for restaurant floors, 50,000–100,000 for lobby and bar. Heavier weave, not heavier coating.
VinylWhere vinyl is specified for cleaning reasons, the failure is at the seam and the piping, not the face. Ask for the seam construction, not only the grade.
COM yieldA 1.4 m fabric runs about 1.6–2.0 m per side chair and 3.5–4.5 m per 1,400 mm two-seat bay, before the repeat is counted. Patterned and railroaded fabrics move those figures.
FireCAL 117 for California, BS 7177 for the UK, IMO for marine. The certificate follows the composite — foam plus fabric plus interliner — not the fabric alone.

How is the frame built, and why does it matter at year five?

A chair is a joint, repeated eight times. Kiln-dried hardwood or a 15-ply structural panel, cut and machined, then mortise-and-tenon at the rail, a corner block at the leg, glue and a dowel. Staples hold a residential chair together for a while; they do not hold a contract chair through a thousand covers a week.

Moisture content is the quiet variable. Timber dried to 8–12% and then shipped to a Gulf Coast lobby or a Dubai atrium will either swell at the joint or open it, depending on which way the destination sits. It is the reason the same model is not built the same way for every market.

Springs follow the same logic: sinuous 8-gauge on the seat where the load is, webbing on the back where it is not. Over-sprung backs are a cost with no service life attached to it.

A test that costs nothing. Lift one chair by a front leg and twist. A jointed frame moves as one piece; a stapled frame creaks before it comes apart. It is the same test a maintenance team runs at month three, and it is cheaper to run it at the factory.

What does a curved banquette change?

A curved run is the one fixed-seating shape that cannot be corrected on site. The radius sets the chord, the chord sets how many covers fit, and the covers at the two ends of an arc are always the ones nobody wants — the seat is deepest where the radius is flattest and narrowest where it turns.

Construction is different too. A curved back is built on CNC-cut plywood ribs rather than a straight frame, the upholstery is cut on the bias and pattern-matched around the curve, and the plinth is templated on site if the wall it sits against is not actually straight — which it usually is not.

The practical alternative for most rooms is a modular bay: straight sections that bolt together, with a curved end unit where the room turns. It installs through a standard door, it can be re-configured when the layout changes, and it costs less than a one-piece curved back that has to be craned in.

Both are available. The seating factories we draw on build curved runs to a radius and modular bays to a bay width, so the choice is a layout decision rather than a sourcing one.

How does seating arrive and get installed?

  • Chairs travel assembled or knock-down depending on the model. Knock-down saves volume and costs site labour; for a 120-cover room that trade is worth doing the arithmetic on.
  • Banquettes travel as bays with the plinth separate. The plinth is levelled first, the bay lands on it, and the fixing is hidden under the seat — which is why the plinth has to be right before the floor protection comes up.
  • Sequence matters more than packing. Booths and banquettes go in before tables and before the carpet is finally cleaned, because a 2 m bay does not go around a fixed table.
  • Labelling is by zone and room number, not by model. A restaurant package sorted by model on site is a week of labour nobody budgeted.

Questions that come up before a seating schedule is released

Can you match a banquette to a designer's elevation?

Yes, if the elevation carries a radius or a plan dimension, the seat height it is drawn to and the back height. What cannot be taken off a picture is the fixing and the plinth, and those are the two items that decide whether the run can be installed after the floor screed is finished.

Do we supply the fabric, or do you?

Either. On COM and COL we return a cutting report with the metres actually consumed and the balance returned, because the waste on a patterned fabric is where a seating budget quietly goes over. On our stock ranges the rub count and fire certificate come with the sample card.

What is the smallest order you will take?

A single booth bay or a single chair model is a production item, not a sample run, so the order is quoted on the model rather than on a container. Mixed models consolidate into one shipment, which is how a 40-cover restaurant ends up with chairs, booths and tables on one bill of lading instead of three.

How long does a seating package take?

The critical path is the fabric approval, not the frame. A frame can be cut in days; a COM fabric that arrives after production has started stops the line. Seating is scheduled against the casegoods so both pack for the same container, and the date moves with whichever approval lands last.
