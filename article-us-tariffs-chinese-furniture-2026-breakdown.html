The Complete Breakdown of US Tariffs on Chinese Furniture in 2026: What Hotel Procurement Teams Need to Know

Published September 3, 2026  |  12 min read  |  By foshanFFE.com Editorial Team

The United States now imposes tariffs totaling approximately 35% on Chinese-manufactured furniture entering the country. For hotel procurement teams evaluating FF&E sourcing decisions, this tariff environment demands a rigorous understanding of cost structures, strategic timing, and the comparative economics of domestic versus imported furniture. With the US hotel construction pipeline standing at 6,020 projects in Q1 2026, the stakes have never been higher.

This analysis provides a complete breakdown of every tariff layer, calculates the real-world cost impact on a typical hotel FF&E project, and outlines proven strategies that procurement teams are deploying to optimize their hotel procurement tariff strategy in 2026.

~35% Total US Tariff on Chinese Furniture in 2026 (MFN + Section 301 + Section 122)

1. The Tariff Breakdown: Three Layers Stacking Up

The China furniture import duty structure in 2026 consists of three distinct components, each with separate legal authority and rate schedules:

Tariff ComponentRateLegal AuthorityStatus
Base MFN Duty3.5%HTS Chapter 94 (HS 9401, 9403)Permanent
Section 301 Additional Duty25%Trade Act of 1974, Section 301Active since 2018
Section 121/122 Reciprocal Tariff10%Executive action, 2025Active
Total Combined Rate~35%—2026 effective rate

Each component stacks on the customs value of the goods. For a shipment of hotel casegoods valued at $500,000 FOB Foshan, the tariff calculation works as follows:

Tariff Calculation Example:

• Base MFN (3.5%): $17,500

• Section 301 (25%): $125,000

• Section 122 Reciprocal (10%): $50,000

• Total tariff burden: $192,500 (approximately 38.5% on declared value)

Effective rate lands in the 35–38.5% range depending on product classification and valuation methodology.

3. Category-by-Category Tariff Impact Analysis

The Section 301 tariff on furniture does not affect all hotel FF&E categories equally. Understanding these differences is critical for building an effective hotel procurement tariff strategy:

Casegoods (Desks, Dressers, Wardrobes)

HS 9403 — the primary classification for wooden hotel furniture — carries the full tariff burden. Casegoods represent the largest share of guestroom FF&E spend and face the steepest cost differential between Foshan and US domestic sourcing. Even at 35% tariff, Foshan casegoods deliver 20–35% savings versus US-made equivalents due to manufacturing scale and material supply chain advantages.

Soft Seating (Lobby Sofas, Guestroom Chairs, Ottomans)

HS 9401 — upholstered seating — carries the same combined tariff rate. However, soft seating faces an additional consideration: CAL TB 117 fire compliance requirements add to manufacturing costs. Foshan suppliers experienced with US fire standards can produce compliant upholstered furniture at significantly lower cost bases than domestic manufacturers.

Lighting Fixtures

Lighting fixtures (HS 9405) face the same tariff layers but benefit from a different dynamic: US domestic lighting manufacturing has contracted substantially, and Foshan — particularly the Dali district — remains the global leader in decorative and architectural lighting. The tariff impact on lighting is partially offset by the near-absence of competitive domestic alternatives.

Mattresses and Bedding

Mattresses present the most complex tariff scenario. Beyond the standard tariff layers, mattresses from China face additional anti-dumping duties (ADD) and countervailing duties (CVD) that can exceed 100% in certain cases. This makes mattresses one of the few categories where domestic sourcing or third-country alternatives may be more economical than direct China import. Smart hotel procurement teams often source mattresses from US-based manufacturers while importing all other FF&E categories from Foshan.

⚠️ Critical Note on Mattresses: Mattresses from China face anti-dumping duties (ADD) and countervailing duties (CVD) that can exceed 100%. These are separate from and in addition to the 35% tariff discussed in this article. Hotel buyers should verify current ADD/CVD rates with their customs broker before including mattresses in China sourcing plans.

4. Four Strategies to Optimize Tariff Costs

Strategy 1: Lock Pricing Early and Time Shipments Strategically

With the Section 301 rate already at 25% and the broader tariff environment subject to further escalation, early procurement delivers tangible savings. Hotel procurement teams with projects scheduled for 2027–2028 openings should consider:

  • Issuing purchase orders in Q3–Q4 2026 to secure current pricing before potential rate increases
  • Negotiating tariff-rate lock clauses with suppliers that tie pricing to PO date rather than shipment date
  • Structuring phased delivery schedules that align with project milestones to avoid premature customs clearance

Early procurement combined with supplier transparency can save 12%–18% of total FF&E budget when factoring in avoided cost escalation and optimized freight scheduling.

Strategy 2: Consolidate Shipments for Freight Efficiency

Tariffs are calculated on the customs value of goods, not on shipping costs — but efficient container loading reduces per-unit freight, which in turn reduces the CIF value on which tariffs are calculated. Working with a single consolidation and global shipping partner who manages the full FF&E scope enables:

  • Full container load (FCL) optimization across all furniture categories
  • Reduced per-unit freight costs through volume consolidation
  • Accurate customs valuation based on consolidated invoicing
  • Single point of accountability for logistics compliance

Strategy 3: Direct Supplier Sourcing to Eliminate Middleman Markups

Tariffs are calculated on the transaction value declared at customs. When procurement flows through intermediaries — trading companies, export agents, or sourcing offices — each layer adds markup that gets included in the customs valuation and therefore gets taxed. Direct supplier procurement through an experienced integrator eliminates these intermediate markups, ensuring that the tariff base reflects actual manufacturing cost rather than inflated transaction values.

Strategy 4: Value Engineering for Tariff-Aware Specification

Working with quality control teams who understand both tariff classification and manufacturing specifications enables tariff-aware value engineering:

  • Knock-down (KD) construction reduces container volume, lowering per-unit freight and CIF value
  • Standardized dimensions across room types reduce SKU complexity and manufacturing costs
  • Material substitutions (HPL vs. veneer, engineered wood vs. solid) maintain aesthetic quality while reducing customs valuation basis
  • Strategic product classification that optimizes tariff rates where multiple HS codes may apply
