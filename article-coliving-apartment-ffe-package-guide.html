Co-living & Apartment FF&E: Complete Furniture Package Guide for Operators and Developers

The co-living, build-to-rent (BTR), aparthotel, and student housing sectors are growing faster than traditional hotel development in most major markets. Unlike hotels — where a single operator manages each room and refurbishes on a 7–10 year cycle — co-living and rental apartments face higher tenant turnover, heavier daily wear, and a need for modular replacement. The FF&E specification must account for this reality.

This guide covers room-by-room configurations, cost benchmarks, material selection, fire compliance, and container logistics for operators procuring FF&E packages from Foshan, China.

How does co-living and apartment FF&E differ from hotel FF&E?

The product categories overlap — beds, desks, wardrobes, seating — but the performance requirements diverge significantly:

Factor Hotel FF&E Co-living / Apartment FF&E
Tenant turnover Daily housekeeping, 1–3 year soft refurb cycle Monthly tenant rotation, furniture must survive 5–10 year hard cycle
Durability demand Moderate (maintained by housekeeping) High (tenant-owned care, no daily maintenance)
Modularity Fixed layout per room type Standardised modules across hundreds of identical units
Replacement strategy Replace by room or floor Replace by unit or component (individual drawer, panel, cushion)
Assembly model On-site assembly crew for full fit-out Flat-pack / KD for tenant self-assembly or rapid turnover crew
Budget per room USD 4,000–12,000 (mid-range to luxury hotel) USD 1,500–5,000 (economy to mid-market co-living)
Fire compliance Hotel-grade (CAL 117, BS 5852) Often stricter (BS 7176 for UK student housing, Crib 5 mandatory)

The core difference: hotel furniture is designed to look good and survive moderate use; co-living furniture must survive heavy abuse at lower cost with faster replacement economics.

What is the optimal material selection strategy?

The material choice directly determines cost, durability, and replacement economics:

Material Cost Index Durability Moisture Resistance Repairability Best Application
Melamine board (三聚氰胺板) Low Good (scratch-resistant surface) Moderate Poor — cannot be spot-repaired; panel replacement required Wardrobes, desk carcasses, kitchen cabinets
Veneer on MDF/plywood (实木贴皮) Mid–High Excellent (can be refinished) Low–Moderate Good — light sanding + re-stain possible Premium casegoods, headboards, feature panels
Lacquer / painted finish (烤漆) Mid–High Good Moderate Moderate — touch-up possible, full refinish requires workshop Accent pieces, kitchen cabinets, vanity fronts
Solid wood High Excellent Variable by species Excellent Premium lobby furniture, feature pieces, dining tables
Laminated high-pressure (HPL) Mid Excellent (impact + scratch) High Poor — panel replacement High-traffic communal tables, kitchen countertops

Recommendation for co-living: Use melamine board for 80 % of casegoods (cost-efficient, replaceable), with veneer or HPL accents in communal areas where appearance and durability matter most. Avoid solid wood for tenant-occupied rooms — the maintenance cost outweighs the aesthetic benefit.

What are the fire compliance requirements by market?

Fire regulations for furnished rental accommodation are stricter than hotels in most jurisdictions, because tenants spend longer hours in the space and are responsible for their own fire safety.

Market Required Standard Scope Key Test
United Kingdom BS 7176 (furniture) + BS 5852 (ignition sources) All upholstered furniture in rented accommodation Crib 5 ignition test mandatory for all foam-filled items
United States (California) CAL 117 (Technical Bulletin 117) Upholstered seating, mattresses Open-flame test for foam and barrier materials
United States (Federal) 16 CFR 1633 + 1632 Mattresses only Cigarette and open-flame (mattress set)
European Union EN 1021 Part 1 & 2 (upholstered seating) Seating furniture in commercial/rental use Cigarette (Part 1) + equivalent butane flame (Part 2)
Australia AS/NZS 3744 + AS/NZS 4428 Upholstered furniture Ignitability testing for foam and fabric combinations
UAE / GCC Civil Defence fire code (varies by emirate) Hotels and serviced apartments Typically references BS or NFPA standards

Practical approach for Foshan suppliers: Most established Foshan upholstery factories can produce to Crib 5 (UK) and CAL 117 (US) specifications using FR-treated foam and barrier fabrics. The key is specifying fire compliance before production begins — it affects foam density, fabric treatment, and unit cost by 8–15 %.

Q1: What is the difference between co-living FF&E and hotel FF&E?

Co-living furniture must withstand higher daily wear from tenants who do not have housekeeping service. Specifications emphasise durability (higher-density foam, reinforced joinery, commercial-grade hardware) over decorative detail. Fire compliance is often stricter than hotels (e.g., Crib 5 mandatory for UK student housing).

Q2: What is the minimum order quantity for a co-living project?

Our integrator model works from 50 units upward. Below 50 units, per-unit costs increase 15–25 % because factory setup and QC overhead cannot be amortised. At 100+ units, pricing reaches optimal range.

Q3: Can we get furniture designed for our specific floor plan?

Yes. Provide apartment layout drawings (DWG or PDF with dimensions), and the design team produces custom furniture sizes to match. Standard widths (600/800/1000/1200 mm) minimise waste, but custom sizes are available at 5–10 % cost premium.

Q4: How do we handle warranty claims across 200+ units?

We maintain a spare-parts inventory (replacement drawer slides, hinges, shelf supports, fabric covers) equal to 3 % of total units. Claims are fulfilled via parts shipment in the next available container — typically within 4–6 weeks of the claim.

Q5: What materials work best for student housing?

Melamine board with PVC edge-banding is the standard for student housing: cost-effective, scratch-resistant, and easily replaced if damaged. Metal bed frames (powder-coated steel) outperform wooden frames in high-turnover student accommodation. Avoid fabric-upholstered seating in common areas — use vinyl or performance fabric with wipe-clean surfaces.

Q6: Can KD furniture feel cheap?

Modern KD hardware (cam-lock, minifix, concealed bracket) produces joints that are structurally equivalent to permanently assembled furniture. The key is specifying 18 mm board thickness minimum (15 mm flexes under load), reinforced corner blocks, and metal-to-wood connection points at stress areas.

Q7: What fire certificates do we need for UK student housing?

All upholstered items must pass BS 7176 (Medium Hazard for student accommodation) with Crib 5 ignition source testing. Mattresses must comply with BS 7177 (Medium Hazard). Curtains and drapes must meet BS 5852 or BS EN 13773. All test reports must come from UKAS-accredited laboratories.
