Hotel FF&E Fire Compliance: CAL 117 vs BS 7177 — What Buyers Need to Know

Published August 29, 2026  |  9 min read  |  By foshanFFE.com Editorial Team

Fire compliance for hotel upholstered furniture is one of the most critical — and most commonly misunderstood — aspects of FF&E procurement. The standards vary significantly by market: the United States follows CAL TB 117 (California Technical Bulletin 117), while the Middle East and many international projects require BS 7177 (British Standard). Getting this wrong can result in failed inspections, project delays, product recalls, and liability exposure.

This guide provides procurement teams with a clear technical comparison of both standards, explains what to specify in FF&E RFPs, and outlines how experienced sourcing integrators manage compliance across multiple markets.

The Two Standards at a Glance

🇺🇸 CAL TB 117 (US Market)

  • Full Name: California Technical Bulletin 117-2013
  • Scope: Upholstered furniture sold in California (de facto national standard)
  • Test Method: Open-flame test for smolder and flame ignition
  • Applies To: Residential and non-contract upholstered furniture
  • Key Focus: Fabric and filling material ignition resistance
  • Complementary: NFPA 262 (method of test for flame travel of wiring/cables in plenums — sometimes referenced for lobby applications)

🇬🇧 BS 7177 (Middle East / International)

  • Full Name: BS 7177:2008+A1:2011
  • Scope: Resistance to ignition of upholstered furniture for non-domestic situations
  • Test Method: Multiple ignition sources (cigarette, match, butane flame)
  • Hazard Levels: Low, Medium, High (based on occupancy type)
  • Applies To: Contract/commercial upholstered furniture
  • Key Focus: Complete furniture assembly (cover + interliner + filling)

Why the Distinction Matters

Many procurement teams assume that fire compliance is a "one test fits all" requirement. In reality, the two standards test different things, require different documentation, and are enforced by different authorities.

CAL TB 117: What US Buyers Need to Specify

CAL TB 117-2013 is the current revision of California's flammability standard for upholstered furniture. While it is technically a California regulation, it has become the de facto national standard — most hotel brands require compliance regardless of project location.

Key Requirements

  • Smolder Test (Section 1): A lighted cigarette is placed on the upholstery fabric over the filling material. The assembly must not continue to smolder after the cigarette self-extinguishes.
  • Open Flame Test (Section 2): A small gas flame is applied to the edge of the upholstery assembly. Char length is measured and must not exceed specified limits.
  • Component Testing: Both the fabric and the filling material must be tested independently, as well as in the final assembly configuration.

What to Include in Your RFP

CAL TB 117 Specification Checklist

  • Require test reports from accredited third-party laboratories (e.g., Intertek, SGS, UL)
  • Specify that all upholstered items — guestroom chairs, headboard upholstery, lobby sofas — must comply
  • Include fabric, filling, and interliner materials in scope of testing
  • Require suppliers to provide compliance certificates with each shipment
  • Reference NFPA 262 if the project involves plenum-rated materials in public areas
  • Include country-of-origin warranty language in vendor contracts

BS 7177: What Middle East Buyers Need to Specify

BS 7177 is more complex than CAL TB 117 because it introduces a hazard classification system. The required compliance level depends on the type of occupancy and the specific application of the furniture.

Hazard Level Classification

Hazard LevelApplicationTest Severity
Low HazardDomestic / residential hotel rooms (some jurisdictions)Cigarette ignition only
Medium HazardHotel guestrooms, bedrooms, offices in commercial premisesCigarette + match equivalent test
High HazardPublic areas: lobbies, restaurants, conference rooms, corridorsCigarette + match + butane burner test

Most hotel projects in the UAE and Saudi Arabia specify Medium Hazard for guestrooms and High Hazard for public areas. Civil defense authorities in both countries may impose additional requirements beyond the base standard.

Critical Compliance Points

  • Complete Assembly Testing: BS 7177 tests the entire upholstered assembly — cover fabric, interliner, filling material, and the substrate (foam over frame). You cannot pass the test with components tested individually.
  • Interliner Requirement: For Medium and High Hazard levels, a fire-retardant interliner between the cover fabric and the filling is typically required. The interliner material and specification must be documented.
  • Permanence of Fire Retardant Treatment: Treatments must be durable — not surface-only spray applications. If the item requires cleaning, the fire retardant properties must survive the cleaning process.
  • Traceability: Each production batch should be traceable back to tested components. Civil defense inspectors may request batch-level documentation.

BS 7177 Specification Checklist

  • Specify the required hazard level (Medium for guestrooms, High for public areas)
  • Require complete assembly testing — not component-level testing alone
  • Specify interliner material and construction in technical specifications
  • Require test reports from UKAS-accredited or equivalent laboratories (SGS, Intertek)
  • Include batch traceability requirements in purchase orders
  • Verify fire retardant treatment permanence (cleanability testing)
  • Require compliance certificates in English and Arabic (for Saudi projects)
  • Include civil defense approval support in supplier scope

The Supplier's Role in Compliance

Fire compliance is not a checkbox at the end of the procurement process — it is a design and manufacturing decision that must be integrated from the earliest specification stage. When working with international sourcing integrators, the compliance process should work as follows:

  1. Specification Review: The integrator reviews project fire requirements (CAL 117, BS 7177, or other local standards) and confirms capability before quotation.
  2. Material Selection: Fire-retardant fabrics, foams, and interliners are selected from pre-tested inventories. The integrator maintains a library of compliant material combinations.
  3. Pre-Production Testing: For large projects, the integrator conducts pre-production assembly testing to confirm the specified combination passes before manufacturing begins.
  4. Production Quality Control: Materials are verified against tested specifications during production. Any substitution (even of seemingly minor components like thread or backing fabric) requires re-testing.
  5. Documentation Package: Test reports, compliance certificates, and batch records are compiled and shipped with the goods for handover to local fire inspectors.

Industry Best Practice

Experienced FF&E integrators maintain pre-tested material libraries for both CAL 117 and BS 7177 compliance. This enables rapid specification and quotation without the 4–6 week lead time required for new testing. When evaluating suppliers, ask about their tested material library and pre-certified combinations.

Common Compliance Mistakes to Avoid

  • Using CAL 117 compliance for Middle East projects: These standards are not interchangeable. BS 7177 tests are more rigorous and test the complete assembly.
  • Testing components instead of assemblies: A fabric that passes individually may fail when combined with a different foam or interliner. Always test the final assembly.
  • Assuming treatments are permanent: Some fire-retardant sprays wash out after one cleaning. Specify durable, integral FR treatments.
  • Overlooking interliner requirements: BS 7177 Medium/High Hazard requires interliners. If your specification doesn't include one, the assembly will likely fail.
  • Substituting materials without re-testing: Even small changes (different foam density, different thread) can affect fire performance. Every material change requires validation.

The Cost of Non-Compliance

The consequences of fire compliance failures extend far beyond the cost of re-testing. Non-compliant furniture at a hotel project can result in:

  • Failed fire inspection and delayed hotel opening (costing thousands per day in lost revenue)
  • Mandatory removal and replacement of all non-compliant upholstered items
  • Liability exposure in the event of a fire incident
  • Damage to supplier relationships and project team reputations

For a 200-room hotel project, the cost of replacing non-compliant upholstered furniture can reach $200,000–$400,000 — far exceeding the incremental cost of proper testing and compliance from the outset.

Summary: A Compliance-First Approach

Fire compliance is not optional — it is a fundamental safety requirement that must be embedded in the procurement process from day one. Whether your project requires CAL 117 (US market) or BS 7177 (Middle East market), the key is early engagement with suppliers who understand the standards, maintain pre-tested material libraries, and can provide complete documentation packages.

For hotel developers and procurement teams, the recommendation is clear: specify fire compliance requirements in your initial RFP, verify supplier capability before placing orders, and maintain documentation traceability from specification through installation.
