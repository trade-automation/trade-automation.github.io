US Customs Binding Ruling for Hotel FF&E: How to Lock Your HTS Code Before the Container Ships

📅 November 2026 ⏱️ 11 min read 📋 Compliance guide
🏦 A hotel FF&E package is 40 to 80 SKUs across six HTS headings — each one a separate classification decision

A hotel FF&E package is not one product. It is casegoods, seating, lighting, mattresses, drapery, mirrors and bath vanities — often 40 to 80 distinct SKUs on a single purchase order. Each SKU carries its own HTS subheading, its own duty rate, and its own exposure if the classification turns out to be wrong four years later.

A CBP binding ruling fixes that answer before the container leaves Foshan. This page covers what a ruling actually protects, how to file one, how many SKUs a single request can cover, and what a misclassification costs under the penalty statute.

What is a CBP binding ruling, and what does it actually bind?

A binding ruling is CBP's written position on how specific goods in a specific transaction will be classified, marked, or treated for origin purposes. It is issued under 19 CFR Part 177.

The binding effect sits in 19 CFR 177.9(a): the ruling binds CBP and all of its officers with respect to that transaction until it is modified or revoked. A port director cannot decide differently when your container arrives at a different gateway.

Two limits matter more than the protection:

Limit #1

Prospective transactions only

Under 19 CFR 177.2(b)(2)(ii)(B), rulings issued by the National Commodity Specialist Division and by service ports are limited to prospective transactions. A ruling cannot retroactively bless a shipment that already cleared. If the goods are on the water, you are filing for the next order, not this one.

Limit #2

Your transaction only

Under 19 CFR 177.9(c), a ruling issued to another importer does not bind CBP with respect to your importation, and you should not assume its principles will apply to a different set of facts.

Can you rely on a ruling someone else obtained?

No. This is the most common and most expensive misunderstanding in the FF&E import chain.

CROSS — the Customs Rulings Online Search System at rulings.cbp.gov — is a public database of every ruling CBP has issued. Finding a ruling on "hotel casegoods from China" classified at 9403.60.80 feels like an answer. It is not a ruling for you.

Under 19 CFR 177.9(c), another importer's ruling does not bind CBP on your transaction. CROSS is research, not protection. Use it to build the argument, then file your own request to get the protection.

The trap inside CROSS. Rulings get modified and revoked. A 2019 ruling you are citing may have been superseded by a later change. Search the ruling number before you rely on it and check the modified or revoked status line. A superseded ruling cited in your own submission is worse than no citation at all.

Why is hotel FF&E harder to classify than a single product line?

A typical guestroom package spans at least six headings, and the disputes repeat across projects:

ComponentTypical headingWhat decides it
Upholstered seating, dining chairs9401Seat versus other furniture
Wooden casegoods (nightstand, desk, dresser)9403.60Wooden furniture, essential character
Lamps, sconces, pendant fixtures9405Lighting function
Mattresses and mattress supports9404Construction — sprung, cellular, or other
Drapery, bed skirts6303 / 6302Made-up textile articles
Mirrors, plain or illuminated7009 or 9405Whether the light is the essential character

Three disputes come up on almost every hotel package.

Built-in versus freestanding. A wardrobe fixed to the wall inside a millwork surround may classify as furniture or as a building component, depending on how it is imported and installed. The answer changes the heading and, under Section 301, the rate.

Illuminated mirrors. A backlit bathroom mirror sits between 7009 for glass mirrors and 9405 for lamps and lighting fittings. CBP resolves this on essential character, which turns on construction facts rather than the product name on the invoice.

Mixed-material casegoods. A nightstand with a solid wood frame, MDF carcass, veneer faces and a glass top is not "wood" in the abstract. Under 19 CFR 177.2(b)(2)(ii), the request must state the relative quantity by weight and by volume and the value of each material. The specialist needs those figures to run the essential character analysis, and rough breakdowns do not satisfy the regulation.

What does a real hotel FF&E ruling look like?

CBP issued ruling N356872 for a hospitality unit from Vietnam: a three-door wooden cabinet with a stand, built to hold a mini refrigerator, coffee maker and microwave, for hotel guest rooms. Construction was glass, solid wood, veneer and medium density fiberboard.

Classification came back as 9403.60.8093, free of duty. The reasoning: the essential character of the unit as a whole was imparted by the wooden cabinet component.

Two things to take from that ruling:

The end use did not decide it. "Made for hotel guest rooms" is context. Essential character came from the physical construction, not from where the product was going to be installed.

Country of origin did not remove the classification question. The goods were from Vietnam, not China. Sourcing outside China takes away the Section 301 exposure. It does not take away the need to get the heading right.

How do you file the request?

The eRulings template at erulings.cbp.gov is the standard route for initial requests to the National Commodity Specialist Division in New York. There is no fee.

Step 1

Search CROSS first

Search the product by commercial name, by the heading you think applies, and by the competing heading CBP might reach for. Read the reasoning, not just the result. If a published ruling already covers your fact pattern, cite it in the request rather than starting from nothing.

Step 2

Build the article description

This is where requests succeed or fail. Per 19 CFR 177.2(b)(2)(ii) you need the full description in imported condition, chief use in the United States, commercial or technical designation, and for multi-material goods the relative weight, volume and value of each component. Add photographs from multiple angles, technical drawings, and a description of the manufacturing process.

Step 3

Argue the competing headings

State the two or three subheadings that could plausibly apply, say which one controls, and walk through the General Rules of Interpretation in order. Most FF&E classifications resolve at GRI 1, on the terms of the headings and the section and chapter notes.

Step 4

Include the required declarations

Every request must state that, to the importer's knowledge, no issue involving the commodity is pending before CBP or any court, and whether advice has already been sought from a CBP office and what that advice was. Leaving these out gets the request rejected before a specialist reads the product description.

Step 5

Submit and track

Accepted formats are doc and docx, xls and xlsx, ppt and pptx, pdf, txt, jpg, jpeg, gif, bmp, png and mp4. ZIP files are not accepted, and a non-compliant submission can be rejected on format alone. If the transmission arrives in good order you receive an acknowledgement with a binding ruling control number within one business day.

Step 6

Supply a sample if asked

CBP requests a physical sample by separate communication and provides shipping instructions. Mark the package with your submission confirmation number so it matches your file. The clock pauses until the specialist has the sample.

How long does it take, and how many SKUs can one request cover?

The National Commodity Specialist Division targets 30 calendar days from receipt. That is a target, not a guarantee. A laboratory report or consultation with another agency adds time, and requests referred to Headquarters are issued by mail within 90 days.

The five-item cap is the constraint that catches FF&E importers. A single request may contain no more than five items of the same class or kind. A 200-room package with 60 SKUs is not one request. It is a dozen requests, grouped by heading and by similarity of construction.

Plan the grouping before you file. Casegoods of similar construction group together. Seating groups separately. Lighting groups separately. Grouping by heading rather than by room type keeps each request inside the five-item limit and keeps the fact pattern coherent enough for a specialist to rule on.

What happens if the classification is wrong?

The escalation follows a predictable sequence: CF-28, then CF-29, then 19 U.S.C. § 1592.

CF-28, Request for Information. CBP asks you to document an entry — classification, value, origin, or trade program eligibility. It is an inquiry rather than a penalty, and it is not optional.

CF-29, Notice of Action. CBP states that it is changing something about the entry. A proposed action gives you a window to respond with evidence. A taken action has already happened and shows up at liquidation as a rate advance.

Section 1592 penalty. Triggered by a material false statement or omission. The statute penalizes the false statement itself, not only the revenue loss — a classification error on duty-free merchandise can still produce a penalty.

Penalty ceilings under 19 U.S.C. § 1592(c), without a prior disclosure:

CulpabilityWith loss of dutiesNo loss of duties
FraudDomestic value of the merchandiseDomestic value of the merchandise
Gross negligenceLesser of domestic value or 4× the duties lost40% of dutiable value
NegligenceLesser of domestic value or 2× the duties lost20% of dutiable value

A prior disclosure under 19 U.S.C. § 1592(c)(4) and 19 CFR 162.74 caps the exposure far lower: interest only on the lost duties for negligence and gross negligence, and one times the lost duties for fraud, or 10 percent of dutiable value where no revenue was lost. It requires disclosing the circumstances of the violation, the entries and ports involved, the material false statements, and tendering the actual loss of duties — before, or without knowledge of, the start of a formal investigation.

CBP's penalty authority generally runs five years. A classification error found in year four reaches back across every entry inside that window.

The arithmetic

What a misclassification costs on a real-sized order

Illustrative only, using round numbers. A 200-room select-service package with a landed value of $780,000, correctly dutiable at 37.5 percent, owes $292,500 in duty. Declared under a wrong classification at zero, nothing is paid. Loss of duties: $292,500.

At the negligence tier the ceiling is the lesser of domestic value or twice the loss, which is $585,000. At gross negligence it is four times the loss, capped at domestic value. At fraud it is the domestic value of the merchandise, which for FF&E resold into a hotel project sits well above the landed figure.

The ruling costs nothing and takes 30 days. That comparison is why the filing is not paperwork.

What should you request from your supplier before filing?

The request can only be as precise as the data behind it. Before filing, ask for:

ItemWhy the specialist needs it
Bill of materials with component weights19 CFR 177.2(b)(2)(ii) requires relative weight, volume and value of each material
Material origin by componentDrives the origin question and, with it, the Section 301 exposure
Manufacturing process by countryOrigin turns on where production steps happened, not where the order was placed
Drawings and photographs, including undersideJoinery and material thickness are visible there, and both feed the essential character analysis
Manufacturer identification codeCBP asks for it where known

A supplier who cannot produce a weight-split bill of materials is telling you something about how well they know their own product.
